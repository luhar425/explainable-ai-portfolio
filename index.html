Current portfolio project

Can We Trust the Answer? Explainability and Validation in AI

A research project and infographic built around one question: how can people assess an AI answer when they cannot fully inspect how it was produced?

Objective

Investigate how users can assess outputs from GPT, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA, models whose builders cannot fully say how a given answer was produced.

Challenge

Explain the difference between a persuasive explanation and evidence of reliable model behavior. A convincing explanation can sound reasonable without accurately describing the process that produced the answer.

Approach

Examine interpretability research alongside validation methods and performance metrics. The result is a four-panel infographic, hand-coded in HTML, CSS, and SVG, in which every figure carries a numbered source.

Career connection
Builds on my experience with automated testing, machine learning evaluation, and Python validation for AI-assisted workflows.
Deliverables
A professional visual presentation, a brief explanatory document, and a referenced source list.
Visual structure
1. Define explainable AI and why it matters. 2. Show the obstacles: model complexity, limited access, and incomplete explanations. 3. Explain the evaluation measures: accuracy, calibration, robustness, and fairness. 4. Present research examples and their limitations.
Skills demonstrated
Research synthesis, evidence assessment, comparative analysis, information design, and technical writing.

Research summary

Why model decisions are hard to explain. A language model stores more concepts than it has neurons, so single neurons respond to several unrelated ideas at once (superposition and polysemanticity).14 The tools that untangle this are expensive and partial: sparse autoencoders can be far larger than the model they explain,13 and Anthropic reports that its circuit-tracing method gives satisfying insight on roughly a quarter of the prompts tried.2 The reasoning a model writes out is not a reliable account either. In tests, models that used a planted hint often admitted it less than 20% of the time.4

What the leading organizations are doing. Anthropic's circuit-tracing research investigates internal mechanisms in Claude 3.5 Haiku, including multistep reasoning and cases where the written reasoning is unfaithful to the underlying process, and its explanations remain partial.12 OpenAI trains deliberately sparse models whose circuits are small enough to read, and measures how well chain-of-thought can be monitored.56 Google DeepMind publishes Gemma Scope 2, a set of sparse autoencoders and transcoders for every layer of Gemma 3, the open-weights sibling of Gemini.7 Meta releases LLaMA weights openly with model cards and guard models such as Llama Guard.8

How validation and metrics create accountability. The HELM benchmark evaluates seven dimensions: accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency. That supports using several measures, not one, when assessing reliability.15 Models are also attacked by red teams, and the results are published in model or system cards. Disclosure can be scored as well: Stanford's index rated Meta 60 out of 100 in 2024 and 31 in 2025. The criteria changed in 2025, and the index measures disclosure, not model accuracy.917 Frameworks such as the NIST AI RMF and the EU AI Act turn these practices into expectations and, in Europe, legal duties.1011

1Explainable AI and why it matters

Explainable AI (XAI) is the set of methods that let people understand why an AI system produced a particular output, in terms they can check.

Explainability
How the system works: the mechanism behind an output.10
Interpretability
What an output means in its context of use.10
Transparency
What the developer discloses about data, training, testing, and limits.

NIST lists seven characteristics of trustworthy AI. Being explainable and interpretable is one of them.10

  • Valid and reliable
  • Safe
  • Secure and resilient
  • Accountable and transparent
  • Explainable and interpretable
  • Privacy-enhanced
  • Fair
  • TrustPeople rely on an answer more sensibly when they can see what it rests on.
  • SafetyEngineers can find the cause of hallucinations, bias, or deception and fix it at the root.
  • AccountabilityA decision that can be traced can be questioned, audited, and corrected.
  • ComplianceRules such as the EU AI Act now require disclosure and documentation.

2Challenges in explainability

A convincing explanation can sound reasonable without accurately describing the process that produced the answer. Five obstacles, in four groups, show why.

  • Features > neuronsSuperposition

    Model complexityNetworks pack more concepts than they have neurons, so one neuron fires for several unrelated ideas. Reading the model neuron by neuron does not work.14

  • About 1 in 4Partial tools

    Incomplete explanationsShare of prompts on which Anthropic says circuit tracing gave satisfying insight. The simplified replacement model it relies on matches the real model's output in about half of cases.12

  • Under 20%Unfaithful reasoning

    Incomplete explanationsHow often reasoning models typically admitted using a planted hint that changed their answer. Written-out reasoning can leave out what actually drove the decision.4

  • 60 to 31Shrinking disclosure

    Limited accessMeta's transparency score out of 100 in 2024 and 2025, while the industry average fell from 58 to 41. The criteria changed in 2025, and the index measures disclosure, not model accuracy.917

  • €15M or 3%Staged rules

    RegulationMaximum EU AI Act fine for general-purpose model providers. Model obligations have applied since August 2025 and transparency duties since August 2026, while high-risk rules were pushed to December 2027.1112

3Validation and performance metrics

An explanation says why. Validation shows whether the model holds up, and metrics say how well, in numbers anyone can compare.

  1. BenchmarkStandard tests of knowledge, code, and truthfulness, such as MMLU, HumanEval, and TruthfulQA.
  2. Measure broadlyScore more than accuracy. HELM evaluates seven dimensions.15
  3. Red teamPeople attack the model with jailbreaks and prompt injection to find failures before users do.
  4. DocumentPublish results and limits in a model card or system card.8
  5. MonitorKeep checking after release, including whether reasoning stays monitorable.6

The seven HELM dimensions. No single number captures reliability, so several measures are used together.15

  • Accuracy
  • Calibration
  • Robustness
  • Fairness
  • Bias
  • Toxicity
  • Efficiency

Six core measures, sorted by the question each one answers.

Is the model reliable?

Accuracy
How often the answer is correct.
Robustness
Whether quality survives rephrased or hostile input.
Hallucination rate
How often it states false things as fact.

Can it be held to account?

Calibration
Whether stated confidence matches how often it is right.16
Fairness
Whether results differ unfairly across groups.
Faithfulness
Whether the explanation matches what the model did.4

Explanatory document

Key points

The infographic asks how people can assess an AI answer when they cannot fully inspect how it was produced, and it answers that trust has to be earned in three ways at once. Explainability shows why a model produced an output. Validation shows whether the model holds up under testing and attack. Performance metrics such as accuracy, calibration, robustness, and fairness put that reliability into numbers that can be compared and audited. For GPT, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA, each of these is incomplete on its own: the best tracing tools work on only a fraction of prompts, written-out reasoning often omits what drove the answer, and public disclosure has been falling while regulation arrives in stages. An explanation without testing is only a story, and a test score without an explanation is a number nobody can question. A model becomes trustworthy and effective when all three are present, which is the point of the final equation.

Design rationale

Each relationship has the chart type that fits it. The validation pipeline is a numbered flow because its steps happen in order. The techniques are a stepped scale because they differ in degree, from observable behavior to internal mechanisms. The four labs share a table because the same attributes, including limitations, are compared across each. The closing chart links every challenge to its response and its check, since cause and response is the relationship that matters most. The graphic starts dark and ends light, so the reader moves from the opaque model to something that can be seen into, and the opening diagrams show the same network twice, first as a tangle and then with one real traced path picked out. Amber marks obstacles and teal marks what has been understood or measured, so color carries meaning. The body typeface, Atkinson Hyperlegible, was designed for legibility, which suits a piece about making things readable. Every figure links to a source dated 2022 to 2026, because a graphic about transparency should itself be checkable.

Classification process and reflection

How I classified the content

I sorted every finding with one question: what job does this item do in helping someone trust an answer? That produced four top-level categories: why explainability matters, what stands in the way, how answers are evaluated, and what researchers are doing. A flat list inside each category hid the patterns, so I added a second level. Obstacles are grouped as model complexity, incomplete explanations, and limited access, with regulation kept as a fourth group because it is an outside pressure, not a property of the model. Evaluation measures are split by the question they answer, either whether the model is reliable or whether it can be held to account. Solutions follow the two approaches in my research, testing observable behavior and investigating internal mechanisms, and they are ordered by depth because depth predicts both how much a technique explains and how much it costs.

The borderline cases needed the most thought. Chain-of-thought monitoring is both a technique and a check, so it appears in both places on purpose. Red teaming went under validation, not explainability, because it shows that a model fails without showing why. Guard models such as Llama Guard filter outputs and do not explain them, so I classed them as behavior tests. Meta's open weights count as a solution, yet Meta's falling transparency score is also my example of limited access, which is why I keep open weights and disclosure as separate ideas. I rejected sorting by company, because it would have hidden the fact that the labs are converging on the same few methods. The comparison table keeps the company view without making it the structure.

Reflection on learning

The hardest part was vocabulary. Sources use explainability, interpretability, and transparency almost interchangeably, and I could not classify anything until I had fixed a definition for each, which is why the first panel opens with definitions. The second difficulty was speed: several key sources were only months old and contradicted older summaries. The strategy that worked was to identify the requirements, consult original research such as lab papers, the HELM paper, and the Stanford index, check each number, and tie every conclusion to a source before starting the layout. Two findings changed my thinking. I had assumed that a model showing its reasoning was explaining itself, and the evidence that this reasoning often leaves out what drove the answer showed me why an explanation has to be validated like any other output. I had also assumed that open weights meant transparency, and Meta's score showed otherwise, with the caution that the score measures disclosure and its criteria changed. The connection to the course concepts is that explainability, validation, and performance metrics are one system, with each covering a weakness in the others. The project also connects to my own work. I have used cross-validation to compare models and Python validation to check AI-assisted workflows, and this research explained why those checks matter: a persuasive explanation is not evidence of reliable behavior. I will carry that distinction into practical AI integration, where an automated test has to stand behind anything a model produces.
